How to stop reformatting CVs by hand

For UK recruitment business owners still formatting CVs into client templates by hand: what the loop costs per person, and how to hand it over with a check still in place.

Last updated 7 September 2026

It's 4pm on a Tuesday. A client emails: great shortlist, but can you send them in our template? The shortlist is finished. The afternoon now has a hole in it, and the same hole turns up again on Thursday from a different client.

That's the loop: reformatting CVs into a client's own template. Copy the details across and match their headings. Then straighten whatever the original file does to your layout. The work was finished on Wednesday. The formatting is what's eating the week.

The maths (a sketch, not a client figure)

The attributed number first. Totaljobs research from 2025 put recruiter admin at an average of 17.7 hours per vacancy, more than two working days per hire, and roughly £17,000 per recruiter per year in lost productivity. A solid slice of that is copying details from one document into someone else's template.

The slice, as a sketch:

  • 10 CVs a week reformatted by hand, at 20 minutes each (inside the 15 to 30 minutes a clean one really takes)
  • = 200 minutes a week, which at £30 an hour is £100 a week
  • × 46 working weeks = £4,600 a year. Per person doing the formatting.

Call it 150 hours a year of selling time, spent copying and pasting. Before someone says it: yes, software exists. Most teams still format by hand, because generic tools choke on a bespoke client template, and fixing their output takes as long as doing it properly.

Score it: the three checks

  • Hours. 4. Two to four hours a week on a busy desk, more in a busy month.
  • Repeat. 5. Every client template has fixed headings and fixed fields. A new starter could follow the whole job from one page.
  • Risk-if-wrong. 4. A slip is a detail in the wrong box, and it gets caught at the approval check before the client sees it.

Verdict: three checks at 4 or more. Hand it over.

Try one thing this week (free)

Next time someone on your team formats a CV into a client template, ask them to run a stopwatch. Note the minutes. Then note the quirks: which headings, which fields, what always needs fixing after. By Friday you'll have the first draft of the one-page version of the job. Most owners have never timed it. They just feel the afternoons disappear.

Where a person stays

AI drafts the reformatted CV. A person approves it in two minutes. Nothing goes to a client unchecked. The check isn't a tax on the automation; it's what makes the hand-over safe. Software prepares, a person decides, and the client gets a CV that reads like your team sent it. Because your team did.

FAQ

Won't clients notice the CVs look machine-made?

They'll notice they look right, because the template is theirs. The approval check is where a person confirms headings, order and details before anything leaves. What no client should ever get is a CV nobody looked at.

What about scanned CVs, tables and borders?

That's where the minutes go, and where the draft step earns its place. Software does the extraction, the person checks the result against the original. Messy files are exactly where an approval check pays for itself.

Doesn't recruitment software already handle this?

Where it does, use it. The loop this post is about is the hand-formatting that still happens around the tools you already use, usually for the clients who send their own template.

What else is inside the 17.7 hours?

Chasing client feedback, updating candidates, retyping details between tools. Score each with the same three checks and start with the one that eats the most hours and is easiest to write on one page.

Do we hand over every CV, or only certain clients?

Only where a client sends their own template. The rest of the week the CV leaves in your own format, which was never a loop. Hand over the narrow, repeating version, not the whole pipeline.
