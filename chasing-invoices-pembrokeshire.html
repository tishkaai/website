Chasing invoices from a job in Pembrokeshire: the admin that follows the work

For trades and small firms in Pembrokeshire: how to put the invoice chase on a rhythm so it stops eating the week, and keep a person approving every message.

Last updated 10 September 2026

The job finished in Haverfordwest in the middle of August. The invoice went out the same week. It's now late September, the money hasn't landed, and the job has grown a second job: chasing. A polite email, a wait, a phone call, a wait, and all the while the next job's materials need paying for out of the same pot.

That's the loop nobody quotes for: the admin that follows the work around. The work was good and the client is decent, and there is still a month of reminders, half-answers and "it's with our accounts department" between the invoice and the money.

The maths (a sketch, not a client figure)

Take the chasing on one book of work: reminders, calls, notes, follow-ups.

  • × 2 hours a week = the time cost
  • × £30 an hour = £60 a week
  • × 46 working weeks = £2,760 a year

Now the same chase with a draft step. The software tracks dates and writes the reminder when an invoice crosses its terms, and a person reads and approves in a few minutes:

  • 15 minutes a week of approving, instead of 2 hours of composing, remembering and chasing.

The money isn't the whole win, either. Consistent chasing gets paid faster than occasional chasing, because the reminder arrives on day 31 rather than whenever someone finds an hour.

Score it: the three checks

  • Hours. An hour or more a week spent remembering and chasing. Most trades clear that without trying.
  • Repeat. The chase follows a pattern: reminder at 7 days, firmer note at 14, call after. If the pattern can be written down, it can be drafted for you.
  • Risk-if-wrong. A reminder sent twice is a small embarrassment. A wrong figure or a threatening tone is not. That's why every draft waits for a person.

Try one thing this week (free)

Write your chase ladder down: what you send at 7 days, at 14, at 30, in your own words. Then check every open invoice against the dates and send the one that's due. That page is the automation spec. Anyone, or any tool, can follow it once it exists, and you'll know within a fortnight whether the rhythm alone speeds the money up.

Where a person stays

Every reminder is a draft until a named person approves it. The software remembers the dates; the human owns the tone and the decision to send, hold or phone instead. Anything disputed goes straight back to a person with the whole history attached. Financial work never auto-sends, and the log keeps the receipt of every approval.

FAQ

Isn't chasing awkward to automate?

The awkward part is composing and remembering, and that's what gets drafted. The approved message goes out in your words, on your rhythm. Clients notice consistency, not automation.

What if the client disputes the invoice?

The moment a reply is anything other than routine, it leaves the queue and reaches a human. The automation handles the pattern, not the argument. Disputes deserve a person from the first message.

Does this change what my clients receive?

No. The drafts mirror your own chase messages, with the dates filled in. You approve before anything sends, so nothing leaves that you haven't read.

We're a small firm. Is this too much machinery for us?

The test is the hours, not the headcount. One person chasing for an hour a week, every week, is a repeating job. It can start with one reminder type and grow only if the first one earns its keep.
