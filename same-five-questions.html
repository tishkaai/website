How much of Monday is the same five questions?

For UK accountancy practices, and any owner whose Monday starts the same way: what the five repeat questions cost each year, and the approve-first fix.

Last updated 14 September 2026

It's 8:40 on a Monday morning and the inbox is the same as last Monday with different names on it. Did you get my records? What do you still need from me? Can you send the invoice? When will the return be ready? Did you get my last message? Five questions, none of them hard, all of them costing focus while the real work waits.

That is the loop this post is about: the same five questions, arriving in a different order, answered from scratch every week. Practices know this Monday better than most, because half the questions come with a deadline attached. Every service business has its own five; a builder's are about quotes and start dates, an agency's about shortlists. The shape never changes: the answers exist, and somebody retypes them anyway.

None of the five is hard. All of them cost attention: they land one at a time, mid-thought, each one pulling somebody out of work that needed them. The Monday morning goes to rewriting answers the team gave last week, while the job that would win the next client sits untouched.

The maths (a sketch, not a client figure)

Sketch it per person doing the answering:

  • 5 questions a day at 4 minutes each to find the answer and type it out = 20 minutes a day
  • × 5 days = 100 minutes a week, about 1 hour 40
  • × £30 an hour (a team member's hour; an owner's is worth more) = £50 a week
  • × 46 weeks = £2,300 a year. Per person answering.

At an owner's or partner's rate of £90 an hour, the same 100 minutes is £150 a week, £6,900 a year. And none of that counts the client who waited until Tuesday afternoon for an answer that existed last week. Swap in your own counts and your own rate. (A sketch, not a client figure.)

Score it: the three checks

Score the loop, re-answering the five, on the three checks:

  • Hours. 100 minutes a week per person in the sketch, before counting the interruption cost (3).
  • Repeat. The answers already exist; each of the five fits on one page with blanks for the name and the number (5).
  • Risk-if-wrong. Most of the five have one right answer, and every draft is read by a person before it sends (4).

Two checks at 4 or more, so the verdict is start narrow. Pick the most-asked of the five, write its answer once, and approve every send for a fortnight. If the drafts hold, add the second question. If a draft is still wrong in week three, the question goes back to a person.

Try one thing this week (free)

On Friday afternoon, ask your team one question: which questions did you answer this week that you had already answered the week before? Have them count. The count times four minutes is Monday's bill, and the top name on the list is the first answer worth drafting. Judge it by Friday: the count sits on one page and it cost you nothing.

Where a person stays

Nothing about this auto-sends. The draft lands in the outbox with figures already pulled from the practice's own records, and a person reads it, edits it and taps approve. Answers with numbers in them stay approval-only, because a figure that reaches a client wrong costs more than the minute it took to check. Deadlines and tax positions get written by a person from the start. Software prepares the reply. The practice decides.

FAQ

Our clients' questions vary too much for template answers.

The five most-asked don't vary. They are the same five in a different order, which is why the team can answer them from memory. Leave the genuinely one-off questions to a person and start with the repeats, where the words barely change from client to client.

Isn't this what our practice software already does?

It stores the answer. Somebody still has to turn the stored data into a reply that reads like a person wrote it, and that step is the one eating Monday. The drafting is the missing layer, which is why the retyping never stopped when the software went in.

What about answers with figures in them?

Figures stay approval-only. The draft shows where each number came from, a person checks it against the records and taps send. No figure leaves unchecked, which is the standard we hold on our own work too.

Will clients be able to tell a machine wrote it?

They will notice the reply arrived in minutes instead of hours, in your voice, checked by you. What clients remember is the wait. If a draft reads wrong, you edit it before it goes, so nothing reaches them unchecked.

How long before the drafts are worth trusting?

A fortnight of approving is usually enough to see which of the five hold. A draft that still needs a rewrite in week three is telling you the question was not as repeatable as it looked, and the three checks will usually say why.
