The paperwork that eats a farm's week, and how to hand it to AI

For farmers in Ceredigion and Pembrokeshire: which parts of the week's paperwork a farm can hand to AI, and the one rule that keeps the records honest.

Last updated 9 September 2026

It's a Tuesday during the autumn rounds. The movement records need bringing up to date. Two supplier invoices sit unprocessed. The vet's paperwork and the medicine records need matching up, and somewhere in the week there is a form with a deadline that hasn't been started. None of it is hard. All of it eats the same chunk of every week, and it always lands on the person who already has the most to do.

That's the loop: paperwork that repeats, that can't be skipped, and that grows every year. A farm doesn't struggle because the work is hard. It struggles because the work is constant, scattered across notebooks, inboxes and the cab of a tractor.

The maths (a sketch, not a client figure)

Take the paperwork hours on an average week: records, forms, invoices, chasing.

  • × 5 hours a week = the time cost
  • × £25 an hour = £125 a week
  • × 46 working weeks = £5,750 a year

Now the same paperwork with a draft step. Software prepares the records, the chases and the reminders, and a person checks and approves in under an hour a week:

  • 1 hour × 46 weeks = about 46 hours a year of human time, instead of 230.

The gap is only real if the check actually happens. Approving is faster than typing, but it isn't free, and it isn't optional.

Score it: the three checks

  • Hours. Four or more hours of someone's week, every week. Farm paperwork usually clears that bar several times over.
  • Repeat. Could a relief worker keep the records going from one written page? If the page can be written, the job can be prepared by software.
  • Risk-if-wrong. A late supplier chase is recoverable. A wrong medicine record or a missed form deadline is not. Score each job separately.

The records and the chases score well. The judgement calls, the subsidy decisions and anything with a legal weight stay human, with the software doing no more than preparing the papers.

Try one thing this week (free)

Pick one repeating piece of paperwork and write the page for it. What starts it, the steps in order, where the numbers come from, what finished looks like. Give the page to someone else and have them do the job once from it. If they can, the job is writable, and anything writable can be drafted by AI and approved by you. If they can't, you've learned the job needs tidying before any tool will help.

Where a person stays

The design is one line: software prepares, a person decides. Drafts queue with the source attached, a named person approves, and nothing goes out unchecked. Records, subsidy paperwork and anything compliance-shaped stay approval-only, always. The farm keeps the judgement. The typing, the chasing and the remembering get handed over.

FAQ

Is a farm too small for this?

The size that matters is the hours, not the acreage. If paperwork takes a real chunk of someone's week, every week, the job is worth scoring. One farm's records look much like another's once they're written down.

What about the records that must be exactly right?

They go last, and they never auto-send. The software assembles the draft from your own records and shows where each number came from. A person checks it before anything is filed or sent. Nothing is submitted unapproved.

Does this work with the software the farm already uses?

The aim is to connect what's already there, not to replace it. Accounting, spreadsheets, email: the brain reads from them and files back into them. No new logins for anyone in the yard or the office.

What about signal and connectivity out in the fields?

Drafts wait in a queue until someone looks at them, so nothing depends on perfect signal at the moment of work. The approval happens wherever there's a phone or a laptop, once a day if that suits.
